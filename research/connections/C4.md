## C4b. Fourier series on $S^1$: a unitary change of coordinates (the origin of your evidence)

$$\mathcal F_{S^1} : L^2(S^1) \rightarrow \ell^2(\mathbb Z), \qquad \hat f(k) = \langle f, e_k\rangle, \qquad e_k(t) = e^{2\pi i k t}.$$

- **Unitary, up to the chosen normalization.** The coefficients are projections onto an orthonormal basis (S2a), and the total squared length is the same on both sides (S2b, Parseval).
- **What is preserved, what becomes simple.** Inner products are preserved. A shift becomes a phase on each mode (S3).
- **Not a rotation of one space.** It maps functions on the circle to sequences indexed by $\mathbb Z$, which is a *different* space. It is **not** the phase-space quarter-turn of C4a, and "$\mathcal F^4 = I$" doesn't apply to it.

## C4a. Fourier transform on $L^2(\mathbb R)$: a rotation

The Fourier transform on $L^2(\mathbb R)$ maps the space to itself. It is a quarter-turn in time–frequency phase space: the harmonic-oscillator flow run for a quarter period.

- Its eigenvectors are the Hermite functions $h_n$, with eigenvalues $(-i)^n$, so $\mathcal F^4 = I$.
- Turning by any angle gives the fractional Fourier transform. That is known but uncarded.
