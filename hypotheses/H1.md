## Your clarifications

From `claude_code_prompt.md` §1b:

- It's not a pattern *between* $\hat f(k)$ and $e^{2\pi i k t}$. What these terms represent *together* is seen everywhere in Fourier series and the Fourier transform.
- A Fourier coefficient is a projection: $\langle f, e^{2\pi i n t}\rangle = \int_0^1 f(t)\, e^{-2\pi i n t}\, dt = \hat f(n)$, so $f(t) = \sum_k \langle f, e^{2\pi i k t}\rangle\, e^{2\pi i k t}$.
- *Premise, not a question for Claude:* "how to get an orthonormal basis when you can't visualize it. There must be a way to find that." It involves eigenvalues. Gate 2, answer 2 narrows "infinite things converge, finite things don't": convergence belongs to a limiting process such as $S_N \to f$ as $N \to \infty$.

From [Gate 2](../gate2_answers.md), answer 7:

- "Beyond Euclidean" means two things: other measuring rules $q_M(x) = x^\dagger M x$ with $M \ne I$, and spaces that can't be seen directly (higher-dimensional, Hilbert/function, Fourier-coefficient, prediction spaces, and Minkowski spacetime).
- "We access many abstract or higher-dimensional geometries through representations." This does not say that every non-Euclidean geometry is "only a representation."
- The visualizations should reveal relationships, especially orthogonality, that exist mathematically even when we can't literally see them.
